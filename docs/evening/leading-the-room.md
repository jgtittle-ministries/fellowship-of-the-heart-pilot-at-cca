# Leading the Room — A Facilitation Evening

*FotH Evening · for the leads · not one of the numbered evenings*

*Anchor: 1 Corinthians 14:26; James 1:19; 1 Peter 4:10*

---

**This evening.** One evening for the people who lead the evenings: the
teen leads, and the parents who serve as safety adults. It trains one
thing, facilitation: leading a room so that the room owns its own
evening. The leader's work is not to deliver the evening but to hand it
over.

**Why now.** The leads have led for months. They have met the evening
that runs long, the round that repeats itself, and the leader who keeps
going alone when the other chair should have stepped in. The training
here is offered a little late on purpose: try first, meet the problem,
then learn the answer once it is wanted. There is a time for telling,
and it comes after the learner has met the problem.

**What facilitation is, and is not.** A facilitator runs a practice as
written: holds the container and the clock, asks, waits, turns the talk
to the room, and hands anything heavy to an adult at once. That is a
skill, and it can be practiced in an evening. Accompanying a person's
inner life, hearing what they disclose and helping them through it, is
something else, and it is never asked of a teen lead. The bright line
in the [Evening Handbook](handbook.md) is unchanged.

**Who leads.** John. No one leads what they have not first practiced,
and this evening is new, so the teen leads take part in it first.
Parents take part too; two of them play the practice moments.

**When.** Outside the numbered evenings, on a date set at a planning
lab. About ninety minutes.

**Bring.** Your journal, and the last run sheet you led from.

## The four moves

Everything tonight practices four moves. They build a room that talks to
itself instead of to its leader.

1. **Ask an open question.** One that has no right answer hidden in it.
   "What stood out to you?" invites; "Didn't you think the second verse
   was the key?" steers.
2. **Wait.** Count to seven, silently, before saying anything else.
   Silence is the room thinking, not the leader failing (Jas 1:19).
3. **Turn it to the room.** When someone answers, don't answer back.
   Hand it on: "What do others think about what she said?" "Has anyone
   else seen that?"
4. **Lean back.** Once the room is talking to each other, get out of the
   way. A leader who has to speak after every person has made the room
   into a star with the leader at the center.

When the talk runs from member to member and not through the leader, the
room has begun to own its work. Paul pictured a gathering where "each one
has a hymn, a lesson" and everything is done for building up
(1 Cor 14:26); each gift is given to serve the others (1 Pet 4:10).

## The two practice moments

Before the rounds begin, John says aloud that two parents will play
moments, so that no one mistakes practice for the real thing. Each
moment is played once, briefly, and then stopped and talked over.

**Something heavy.** In role, a parent says something heavier than a
teen should carry, for example: "Honestly, I've been really low lately,
worse than I've let anyone see." The leader's move: thank them, don't
dig, and hand it to the adult at once: "Thank you for telling us. [The
safety adult] will sit with you after." Handing off is leading well, not
failing.

**Something that isn't right.** In role, a parent says something a
parent knows is not right, for example: "I heard God tell me I don't
need to read the Bible anymore." The leader's move: "We hear that," and
on, with no debate and no correction in the circle. Correction moves to
afterward, privately and gently, by a parent or the safety adult, as the
handbook sets out in *When something said isn't right*.

## The pair agreement

Each leading pair writes down, in its own words:

- **How we hand the evening back and forth.** Who opens, who teaches,
  where the handoffs fall.
- **What the second chair does if the first keeps going alone.** A signal
  we agree on, and the words the second chair will use to pause the room.
  The leader asks for this; the second chair does not have to take it.
- **Who keeps the clock.** The container holder, with an adult's
  two-minute signal.

A pair that has agreed beforehand that the other may stop them has made
stepping in a gift, not a challenge.

## How we will know it helped

The question is set before the evening, so the answer cannot bend to fit
it: **in the next two evenings, does the talk run from member to member
more often?** The drawing from 7:05 is the starting point. The leaders
already watch for that kind of talk, and the answer goes in John's notes
of the debrief. No notes are kept on any one person.

## Watch-fors

**No grading.** Nobody is scored and nobody is compared. A round that
goes badly is the evening working: it is what the next fifteen minutes
are for.

**Feedback, leader first.** As in every evening, the one who led answers
first. Receiving correction is the strength being trained.

**Short rounds.** The practice rounds are about the move, not the
content. Keep the questions simple, so the room's attention is on how the
talk moves.

**The practice moments stay practice.** If something real surfaces
during the evening, stop the practice. The safety adult takes it, as on
any evening.
