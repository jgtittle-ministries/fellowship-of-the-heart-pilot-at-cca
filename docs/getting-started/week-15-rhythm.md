## WEEK 15 — THE RHYTHM AND THE FOUR QUESTIONS (90 minutes)

**Aim.** Install the long walk before the sending, and commission the bravest asking of the year. The series' practices only matter if they survive the series — tonight gives every participant the rhythm that carries them — and then hands the parents four questions to ask at home, and the posture to receive the answers without defending.

**Anchor scripture.** Galatians 6:9 ("And let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up") for the rhythm; Psalm 139:23–24 — the "Search me, O God" prayer the room prayed vertically in Weeks 3–4, now asked horizontally — for the questions. Secondary: James 1:19 (quick to hear, slow to speak).

**Connect focus.** All four (sustaining) in the rhythm build; Others — the family bond itself — in the four questions. Nothing new is opened in the room tonight; the deepest opening is commissioned to happen at home, by the willing.

**Mode.** Shared teaching; FAMILY CLUSTERS for the Rhythm Card build — 2–3 whole families per circle with a Cluster Companion, own family always together; whole room for the Four Questions presentation, the Leader Feedback Round, and closing. If total attendance is about ten or fewer, skip the clustering — the whole room stays as one circle throughout.

**Center.** Two movements. (1) Each participant builds their own Rhythm Card — the daily, weekly, and monthly practices they are actually taking with them, in their own handwriting, honest not idealized. (2) The Lead Companion presents the Four Questions — four questions a parent asks their teen (and, where married, their spouse), then listens and receives without defending — tells their own story of asking them, and commissions the at-home practice for the willing. Any unfinished Week 13 gifts-and-mission work completes at the open.

**Who leads.** Teen-led marquee for the rhythm arc — the fourth and final senior-led block. The Companion-in-Formation carries the opening, the long-walk teaching, and the Rhythm Card build from their facilitation card (H15.1). The Four Questions block belongs to the Lead Companion by design: it ends in their own story, and the story is the teaching.

**Between-session practice.** For the willing: the parent asks their teen the four questions at home — unhurried, no audience, phone away — and receives the answers without defending. Married parents are encouraged to also ask their spouse, separately. And everyone runs the Rhythm Card as built, for real, across the break, noting in the journal where it held and where it slipped.

**IJH source.** Vol 2 (the four Connects as a sustained rhythm; the obedience-channel law in community form); the shared Rhythm Card. The four-questions practice is adapted from exercises in the Wild at Heart stream of men's ministry (John Eldredge).

## WATCH FOR (Week 15 specific risks)

- The idealized Rhythm Card. All the daily boxes checked, PROAPT twice a day, a two-hour sabbath plan from a fifteen-year-old with an exam-season schedule. Coach honesty in the clusters: "Write the rhythm you will actually keep, not the one that impresses you."
- The teen leader turning the lead into performance. This is their final in-cohort lead, and the temptation is to be impressive rather than clear. Coach in the prep: the card does the work; your job is to hand it over plainly.
- The family for whom the school year's breaks land hard — a house where holidays and breaks are the dry season. Name the gap explicitly and warmly from the front; let the Cluster Companions watch for who flinches when it is named.
- The parent visibly shaken by the anticipation of asking — the one who goes pale during the presentation, already hearing the answer to question three. That parent needs a Companion before they leave tonight: waiting counts, a letter counts, and "not yet" is an honorable answer. Nobody asks before they are ready to receive.
- The teen who looks alarmed at the homework — the one hearing "my parent is going to interrogate me this week." The willing-only guard is said plainly from the front, to the teens directly: nobody has to answer anything; the pass rule lives at your kitchen table too. If the alarm survives the guard, the Cluster Companion repeats it 1:1 before the family leaves.
- The parent who wants to pre-litigate — "what if the answers aren't fair?" The answers are not a court record; they are what it has been like to be on the other side of you. The only response that keeps the door open is receiving. If fairness matters more than hearing, that parent should wait.

## CRISIS CONTINGENCIES (Week 15)

*Week 15 is lower-risk than Weeks 3–6 — the in-room work is scripted and low-disclosure by design — but it is not zero. Presenting the four questions can land hard on a parent carrying real regret, or on a teen carrying a real wound, right there in the room. Standard Section 6 protocols apply.*

**If the presentation visibly lands on someone mid-block.** The handoff runs on the same protocol rehearsed all series, with one difference: tonight the Lead Companion is the one presenting, so a second adult Companion (named at team pre-work) moves quietly to sit beside the person. The presentation continues. Do not stop the session. Do not spotlight the person. The adult stays with them through the close and opens the follow-up conversation before the family leaves.

**If a teen says, tonight or this week, "I don't want to be asked."** That settles it — willing-only runs in both directions, and no Companion ever brokers a reluctant asking. The Companion's word to the parent is simple: wait. The question set will keep; the trust it needs is still being built, and waiting *is* the four-questions posture.

**If anything surfaces that touches self-harm, abuse, or crisis.** The Companion-in-Formation hands it to the adult immediately (Section 11.6, rule 2) and the full Section 6 protocol runs. The four questions are for the ordinary wounds of family life; anything past that boundary belongs with the Companions and the backup, and every teen has that confidential channel regardless of what is or is not asked at home.

**Default:** Section 6 of the Handbook covers anything that crosses the safety threshold.

# **Session at a Glance**

## **Why this session, this week**

Twelve weeks of practices are sitting in this room, and every one of them has been carried by the room — the Wednesday gathering, the faces, the shared momentum. The room will not meet forever. Most falling-away in formation work happens not in the middle of a series but at its edges, and the biggest edge this cohort will ever face is coming: the year's end, when the weekly room stops and will open a gap between the weekly rhythm and the rest of their lives. Tonight exists so that what the cohort carries across that gap is not momentum — momentum does not survive gaps — but a rhythm, built by their own hand.

And tonight is when the series pays out something it has been quietly training since September. Every week since Week 1, whoever led the evening has stood at the end and asked the room "what would I do differently?" — and received the answers with nothing but "thank you." The cohort has watched adults take honest feedback undefended, week after week, until the posture stopped looking remarkable. The four questions are that same posture at family depth: a parent asking their own teen *how have I wounded you?* and receiving what comes back without correcting the record. The Leader Feedback Round was the rehearsal; the kitchen table is the performance. That is what makes question three survivable — the room has already seen, thirteen weeks running, what it looks like to hear a hard answer and say only "thank you."

And it is why the fourth teen-led marquee is this session. The Companion-in-Formation who carries the rhythm arc tonight is leading the cohort for the final time before their Week 22 commissioning — and then sitting down to watch the Lead Companion model the very thing their whole formation has been about: an adult with nothing to defend.

## **The Four Questions (the asking the room must leave with)**

Asked by the parent, to their teen — and, where married, also to their spouse, separately. In this order:

1. **What has it been like to live with me all these years?**
2. **What have been my messages to you?**
3. **How have I wounded you?**
4. **What would you like from me going forward?**

The parent asks, then listens, then receives. The only responses that belong to the asker are listening questions and "thank you." No correcting the record, no litigating, and never — not this week, not in February — punishing an answer later. Psalm 139:23–24 prayed to God is worship; asked of your own family, it is the bravest form of the same prayer. James 1:19 is the whole method: quick to hear, slow to speak.

# **Pre-Work for the Companion Team**

## **Personal pre-work**

Every Companion reads the Rhythm Card this week — slowly, for themselves, not for the session. Then each Companion answers two questions privately:

Question one — Where is my own rhythm, honestly? Which practices are alive, which have lapsed, and did I grieve the lapsed ones or not notice? You will be coaching honesty in the clusters; the coaching only lands from someone who has just been honest.

Question two — Could I ask the four questions in my own house? Sit with each of the four, and notice which one your stomach tightens on — that is the one the parents in the room will tighten on too. A Companion who has done the asking, or is honestly reckoning with why not yet, is worth ten who present it as an assignment.

**The Lead Companion's pre-work is the story.** The Four Questions block ends in the Lead Companion's own telling — what it was like to ask these questions in their own family, and what came back. That telling is the teaching; the founder's own telling is the model. Prepare it as five unhurried minutes: honest about the fear before, honest about at least one answer that cost something to receive, honest about what it built. No polish, no moral tacked on the end.

## **Block 3 — The Long Walk: Galatians 6:9 (7:15–7:25, 10 min)**

Read the passage aloud. Slow. The teaching is short and it frames the build.

*"And let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up."*

— Galatians 6:9 (ESV)

### **Teaching points (Companion-in-Formation, from H15.1)**

• Galatians 6:9 has a condition in it, and the condition is not "if we feel it." It is "if we do not give up." Paul assumes the weariness. The verse is not for people who never get tired; it is written *to* people who get tired, which is all of us. The reaping is tied to the season, and the season is God's — our part is the not-giving-up. That is what a rhythm is for. Nobody white-knuckles their way through years; people with a rhythm walk through years.

• Here is the plain truth about the next few weeks. The break is coming. For twelve weeks these practices have been carried by this room — by Wednesday, by these faces. Over the break there is no Wednesday. What carries the practices across a gap is not momentum; it is a rhythm you built on purpose. So we build it tonight, before the gap, not after it.

## **Block 5 — Building Your Rhythm Card (7:30–7:50, 20 min)**

Each family cluster works H15.2 — 2–3 whole families with their Cluster Companion, own family always together. A family building rhythms side by side is the design working: the parent writing an honest card next to the teen writing one is half the coaching done already. (If the room stayed as one circle tonight, the same build runs whole-room, with the Companions circulating.) Every participant marks their daily, weekly, and monthly practices — specific times, specific people, specific days — in their own handwriting.

## Cluster Companion: how to coach the build

- Push for specificity, gently. "Morning question" is a wish; "morning question, 6:50, kitchen table, before my phone" is a rhythm.
- The weekly partner line is the most important line on the sheet — the Rhythm Card says so itself. Every participant names an actual person. For teens the parent is a legitimate first answer; so is a cohort friend from this room.
- Coach *down*, not up. The most common error tonight is over-building. "You wrote five daily practices. Which two survive exam season? Start there. The card lets you add later."
- Watch for the participant who writes nothing on the weekly line. That is usually not forgetfulness; it is the belief that nobody would want the job. Do not fix it in the cluster — note it for follow-up.
- If a build conversation surfaces something heavy, honor it briefly and hand it to the adult after the cluster closes. Companions-in-Formation assisting in clusters: rule 2 applies as always.
- Finish with each participant reading their daily line aloud to the cluster — spoken commitments hold better than silent ones. A parent reads first, arranged with that parent before the session — never a cold call on a teen. Teens choose their own depth after; pass-anytime holds.

## The handoff (rehearsed at team pre-work)

Presenting the four questions can land visibly on someone mid-block — a parent hearing question three arrive years too late, a teen hearing it arrive right on time. This is the session working. The protocol runs as every week, with tonight's one difference: the Lead Companion is presenting, so the second adult Companion named at pre-work moves quietly to sit beside the person. No announcement, no spotlight. The presentation continues. The adult stays with the person through the close and opens the follow-up conversation before the family leaves. The 48-hour follow-up stands regardless. The senior's job, as always, is never to hold the person — rule 2 is the bright line, tonight and at FC1.

## **Block 7 — The Leader Feedback Round (8:13–8:21, 8 min)**

*The evening's leader closes the working part of the evening the same way every week.* Tonight a teen led the rhythm arc, so the senior answers first, the adult second — and because this is the Companion-in-Formation's final in-cohort round before their Week 22 commissioning, it also looks back across all four leads, not just tonight.

## Structure (one round — teen first, adult second)

1. The senior: *"Before we close — two things from me, same as every week. What I think went well tonight: ______. What I'd do differently next time: ______."*
2. The Lead Companion answers the same two questions, briefly, for the parts of the evening they carried.
3. Then the room — same two questions about tonight. What went well? What would you do differently? Two or three voices on each question is plenty. The leaders receive without defending — "thank you" is the whole response; the room's feedback covers them both.
4. Because this round is the last one of the series, the Lead Companion closes it by naming one thing the team has watched grow across all four leads: Story II, PROAPT II, the Garden walk-through, and tonight's rhythm arc. Name the growth specifically; the cohort should hear the distance traveled.

# **Coaching Notes: Teens and Parents**

*One high-school band, with their parents beside them in the clusters. These notes travel with the Cluster Companions.*

## **Teens**

For the teens this session is live equipment on one side and a strange gift on the other: the rhythm goes with them to the dorm, and the four questions mean their parent may be about to hand them a microphone no teenager expects.

## Adjustments

- The build: name the horizon plainly — "this card is what goes with you to a dorm." The card they build tonight should survive a semester, which means coaching against the impressive version is most important here. Exam seasons are the honesty test; ask about them directly.
- The four questions, teen side: the willing-only guard is said from the front, but watch for the teen it doesn't reach — the one who looks cornered anyway. A quiet word before the family leaves: "You choose what to answer. Pass is a full answer at home too. And if you're asked and it goes somewhere heavy, the Companions are still your channel." That last clause matters — the §6 door stays open regardless of what happens at any kitchen table.
- The teen who plans to protect the parent — soft answers, everything's fine. Do not coach them toward severity; their depth is theirs to choose, tonight and at home. But if it comes up, one sentence helps: "An honest answer, kindly said, is the best gift on that list."
- Expect the question "can I ask them these questions back?" The answer: the questions belong to whoever is brave enough to ask them and ready enough to receive the answers — but nobody owes anyone an asking, in either direction. Invitation, never demand.

## **Parents**

Tonight may serve the parents more deeply than any session in the series — and it will cost some of them more than any session in the series. Both of those at once.

## Adjustments

- The build: the parent temptation is the over-built card (guilt inflates ambition) or the deferred card ("my rhythm can wait until the kids are older"). The Rhythm Card's own scope rule answers both: keep what is doable in this season; return to the rest when the season changes.
- The four questions: expect three kinds of parents. The ready one — already resolved to ask before Block 6 ends; confirm the guards, especially unhurried and no audience. The shaken one — pale by question three, already hearing an answer; find them before they leave: waiting counts, a letter counts, and receiving guard two matters more than calendar speed. The armored one — "my kids have nothing to complain about"; do not argue; the card goes home anyway, and the story they heard tonight does its own slow work.
- Guard two is the parent coaching hill to die on: never correct the record, never litigate, never punish an answer later. A single litigated answer teaches a teen that honesty has a price, and it can undo twelve weeks of container trust in one evening. A parent who is not ready to keep guard two should wait — say so plainly and warmly.
- The breaks: for some families the holidays and school breaks are the dry season — grief anniversaries, hard relatives, empty chairs. Naming the gap warmly from the front gives those parents permission to build a break-shaped rhythm, smaller and more honest than their term-time one — and permission to hold the asking for a calmer week. The questions keep.

# **Appendix — the Dry-Season block (taught at The Long Walk, Week 20)**

*In this year's sequence the dry-season block is taught at The Long Walk (Week 20), by the Companions-in-Formation as their final rep before commissioning. A body running the fifteen-session edition (git tag `gs-v1.0`) teaches it tonight as originally designed, extending the evening or trimming the rhythm build.*

*Everything below is preserved intact from the original Week 15 design: the Signs Card teaching (the four kinds of dry), the Path Home Card filing, their scripts, and the risk and coaching material that travels with them. When this block runs, the Signs Card and Path Home Card are reprinted from the shared materials, one per participant folder (docs/shared/signs-card.md, path-home-card.md), and the teaching leader's arc adds the two steps at the end of this appendix to their facilitation card.*

## **Anchor scripture for this block**

Psalm 42:1–5 (the downcast soul that still hopes):

*"As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. … Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation."*

— Psalm 42:1–2, 5 (ESV)

### Teaching points (Psalm 42, delivered with the Galatians long-walk teaching when this block runs)

• Notice something strange: this man is dry — panting, downcast, in turmoil — and this psalm is in the Bible. God put the dry season in His own songbook. The psalmist does two things at once: he tells the truth about being cast down, and he preaches hope to his own soul. Both at the same time. That is the posture of the long walk — honest about the dryness, stubborn about the hope.

• And notice which way his desire points. He is dry, and he is *thirsty for God*. Hold onto that; it is the key to everything in this teaching.

## **Why this block exists**

The Signs Card must be learned before the dry season and not improvised inside it, because two of its four cases command opposite motions. The Path Home Card must be placed in every folder while everyone is in the room to hear what it is for, because the person who eventually needs it will, by definition, not be in a room like this when they do. And the Companion-in-Formation who teaches the four kinds of dry is carrying the most important pastoral equipment an FC1 will ever hold — because misreading a dry season is the first hazard of peer leadership.

## **The four kinds of dry (from the Signs Card — the sorting the room must leave with)**

One question sorts everything: **which way does the desire point?** Not *how do you feel* — feelings are exactly what have gone quiet. When you are honest, do you still *want* God — do you miss Him, does the distance ache — or has the wanting itself faded and the world tastes fine without Him?

| | **Desire for God** | **The tell** | **First step** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Neglect | Faded; the distance doesn't ache | No ache at all | Wake and turn; rebuild the rhythm (James 4:8) |
| 2. Worn out | Buried under duty; you want escape, not God | The "I'd be better off elsewhere" whisper | Rest; lighter yoke; stay put; smaller faithful steps (Matt. 11:28–30) |
| 3. The enemy's dry wind | Under assault; you feel accused and pressured | A voice counseling change: *quit, undo, leave* | **Resist**: change nothing, pray a little more, tell someone (1 Pet. 5:8–9) |
| 4. God's own stripping | Intact and starving — the ache is the sign | Painful worry about failing God — lukewarm hearts never worry about that | **Receive**: wait, stay faithful, stay accompanied (Job 23:10) |

Cases 3 and 4 command opposite motions — one says *fight*, the other says *yield* — which is why this must be learned before the dry season and not improvised in the dark. And when you cannot tell which one you are in, the card's own answer applies: do not decide alone. That is what Companions are for.

## **The Signs Card taught (script — 15 min, whole room, whiteboard ready)**

*This is the center of the teaching lift when this block runs. IJH source: A Church Prepared for Revival, Movement III ("When the Ground Goes Dry" — the full teaching behind the Signs Card).*

*"Take out the Signs Card. Here is why this card exists. Sooner or later, everyone who works the Rhythm Card hits a season where the practices go gray. Prayer feels like talking to the ceiling. Scripture reads like cardboard. When that season comes, the most dangerous thing in the room is not the dryness — it is the wrong diagnosis. The voice that says dryness means failure, or fake faith, or a God who looked at you and left. Whole journeys have ended over a misread dry season. This card exists so that never happens to anyone in this fellowship."*

*"The card says dryness is usually the tide, and only sometimes the thorn. The tide goes out and comes back — that is how seas work, and how souls work. This card is for telling the tide from the things that are not the tide."*

*(Write on the board:)* **Which way does the desire point?**

*"One question sorts everything. Not 'how do you feel' — feelings are exactly what have gone quiet. The question is underneath the feelings: when you are honest, do you still want God? Do you miss Him — does the distance ache? Or has the wanting itself faded, and the world tastes fine without Him?"*

*(Walk the four columns on the card, briefly — about a minute each:)*

*"Four kinds of dry. One: neglect. The desire has faded and the distance doesn't ache — the practices lapsed and you don't grieve them. First step: wake and turn; rebuild the rhythm. Two: worn out. The desire is buried under duty — you want escape, not God. First step: rest; a lighter yoke; stay put; smaller faithful steps. Three: the enemy's dry wind. You feel accused and pressured, and there is a voice counseling change — quit, undo, leave. First step: resist. Change nothing, pray a little more, tell someone. Four: God's own stripping. The desire is intact and starving — the ache is the sign. You keep showing up and it feels like nothing, and you are worried about failing God. Lukewarm hearts never worry about that. First step: receive. Wait, stay faithful, stay accompanied."*

*"Now look at three and four, because this is why we learn this card tonight and not in the middle of a dry season: they command opposite motions. One says fight. The other says yield. Getting them backwards is the costliest mistake in the dry country. And when you cannot tell which one you are in — the card's own rule — you do not decide alone. That is what Companions are for."*

*"Four rules on the card. This card is for your own heart, and for one trusted person helping you read it — never for diagnosing somebody else; nobody gets filed under a column. In every case, tell someone — darkness spoken aloud loses half its weight. Make no big decisions in the dark. And know when to call the doctor — if the gray has swallowed everything, sleep and appetite and humor, that is a treatable illness, not a spiritual verdict, and getting help is obedience."*

*"And here is the sentence I most want you to carry out of this room. The card says it plainly: the fact that His absence hurts you is the evidence that He has not gone. Drifting hearts do not ache. Abandoned fields do not thirst. Remember Psalm 42 — the man panting for God is dry and thirsty at the same time, and God put his song in the Bible. A dry season is not a verdict."*

## **The Path Home Card (script — 8 min, whole room)**

*"One more card, and then we are done teaching. Take out the Path Home Card."*

*"Here is what it is. Every formation journey worth taking has people who fall away from it. Some drift. Some burn out. Some blow it — really blow it — and conclude that a room like this one would not want them back. And most churches, without meaning to, leave the way back unwritten: shelves of material for starting the journey and not one page for returning to it. So the returner improvises the hardest walk of their life, alone, guessing at the reception."*

*"Not here. This card is the written door. Five steps: come as you are, and learn to tell conviction from shame — conviction is specific and hopeful and draws you toward home; shame is vague and hopeless and drives you away, and the voice telling you the door is barred is not the doorkeeper's voice. Tell it straight — to God in plain words, and to one trusted person; you choose the person and how much. Grieve what needs grieving — lament is faith with tears in its voice, refusing to leave the conversation. Return to the table — small; the Rhythm Card's own rule was written for this moment: do not start over from the beginning, begin again on the next available day; five dry minutes tomorrow morning is a full return. And let it be celebrated — because in the Lord's own story of return, the ending is not a probation meeting. It is a feast. The father sees his son a long way off, and he runs."*

*"Now — why are we handing you this tonight, when nobody in this room has gone anywhere? Because the person who eventually needs this card will not be sitting in a circle like this one when they need it. They will be far off, sure the door is locked. So we file it now, while everyone is here to hear me say it: the door is not locked. The light is on. If a day ever comes when it is you — a month away, a year, a decade — this card will still be in your folder, and it will still be true."*

*"Everyone take the Path Home Card and put it in your folder. Right now, while we watch each other do it. That filing is a promise this room is making to every person in it."*

### Notes

If "planning for failure" resistance surfaces (it usually comes from a parent), the reframe: a fire extinguisher is not a plan to burn the house down. We mount it on the wall because we intend to live in this house a long time.

## **Watch-fors that travel with this block**

- The participant for whom "the dry season" is not hypothetical — the teaching describes their current life. Expect at least one. This is the session working, not failing: the card exists so that this person hears "not a verdict" while the room is still warm. The adult Companion follows up within 48 hours.
- Treating the Path Home Card as morbid — "why are we planning for failure?" Reframe: a fire extinguisher is not a plan to burn the house down. We put it on the wall precisely because we intend to live here a long time.
- The participant who wants to use the Signs Card on somebody else — a parent sorting their teen into a column, a teen diagnosing a friend. Rule 1 of the card: it is for your own heart, and for one trusted person to help you read it. Nobody gets filed under a column.
- The teen who intellectualizes the four columns — sorting hypothetical people instead of sitting with the question themselves. Rule 1 of the card, gently: it is for your own heart first.
- The teen for whom the desire question lands with a thud — the one who is honestly not sure the wanting is still there. That is a follow-up conversation, unhurried, that week. It is far more often case 1 or 2 than they fear, and discovering that with a trusted adult is the card working.
- The younger teen who hears the dry-season teaching and worries retroactively — "was that time I didn't feel anything at camp the bad kind?" Reassure simply: the ache question, and the reminder that worrying about it is itself the good sign.
- Parents: expect recognition — a parent realizing that a gray stretch of years finally has a name and was never a verdict. Receive it; do not process it in the room. The 1:1 follow-up offer stands for parents too. And some parents carry old departures — their own, or a grown child's. The Path Home Card's word to Companions applies to parents doubly: the father's posture, not the older brother's.
- Some teens know people who have already left — an older sibling, a friend. Expect the quiet question "can I give this to someone?" The answer is yes; the card is a door, and doors are for sharing.

## **Crisis contingency that travels with this block**

**If a participant says, in the room or after, "I think my dryness is the bad kind."** Do not diagnose. Ask the card's own question, gently: "When you're honest — do you still miss Him? Does the distance ache?" Receive whatever comes without a verdict. Then schedule the follow-up: "Let's sit down this week and read the card together slowly. That's exactly what it's for." The sorting happens in an unhurried conversation with one trusted adult — never in the room, never on the spot.

**If the dry-season teaching visibly lands on someone mid-block.** The handoff is scripted: the teaching leader finishes the teaching point; an adult Companion moves quietly to the person; the teaching continues. Do not stop the session. Do not spotlight the person.

## **Facilitation-card additions when this block runs (for the teaching leader's arc)**

1. **Teach the Signs Card.** The wrong diagnosis is the danger, not the dryness. The tide and the thorn. Write the sorting question on the board: **Which way does the desire point?** Walk the four columns — neglect / worn out / the enemy's dry wind / God's own stripping — with each one's tell and first step. Land hard on 3 and 4: opposite motions, never sorted alone. The four rules. The sentence to send home: *the fact that His absence hurts you is the evidence that He has not gone.*
2. **Show and file the Path Home Card.** The written door: conviction vs. shame; tell it straight; grieve what needs grieving; return small — begin again on the next available day; let it be celebrated. The father runs. File it in every folder, witnessed. *The door is not locked. The light is on.*

### If someone asks a Companion-in-Formation, "is my dryness the bad kind?"

You do not answer. You are not the sorter — not that night, not at FC1. You say: *"That question has a card, and it deserves better than a hallway answer — let's get [adult Companion's name]."* And you walk them there yourself. Handing it over well IS the pastoral skill.

### If the Path Home Card gets called morbid

*A fire extinguisher is not a plan to burn the house down. We put it on the wall because we intend to live here a long time.*

## **Pre-work that travels with this block**

Every Companion reads the Signs Card and the Path Home Card slowly, for themselves, before the session where this block runs — and answers privately: which of the four kinds of dry have I been in, and when? Every adult on the team has walked at least one of the four columns. Know your own answer beforehand. If a participant's dry season surfaces, the most useful sentence in the room may be your own, briefly and without drama: "I have been in that column. Here is what the way through looked like."

## **Debrief prompts that travel with this block**

- Every participant can answer "why is a dry season not a verdict?" with something like the card's own answer — the ache is the evidence, the desire question does the sorting.
- The Path Home filing landed as a warm promise, not a strange ritual. Watch the room during the filing; you can see which one it was.
- If the four kinds of dry landed as a quiz to memorize rather than a mercy to keep — if conversation afterward was about sorting other people — re-teach rule 1 at the next gathering.
- Who flinched during the dry-season teaching? The person the handoff went to, and the person it did not — anyone who went still, went quiet, or went to the restroom during the teaching. Every name on this list gets contact within 48 hours.
- Anyone whose dryness surfaced, visibly or quietly — within 48 hours, with the card in hand and no verdict in the room.
